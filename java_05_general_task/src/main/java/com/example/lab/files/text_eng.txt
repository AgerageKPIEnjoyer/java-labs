Over the past fifteen years, empirical science has faced an uncomfortable fact regarding its own practice: a significant proportion of published results have not been corroborated during independent replication.
Large-scale replication projects in psychology have shown that only a third to a half of originally reported effects can be successfully replicated; similar trends have also been found in preclinical medicine and, to a lesser extent, in sociology [2, p. 4].
This "replication crisis" is not a collection of isolated errors inherent only to careless laboratories; John Ioannidis, based on formal statistical calculations, proved that given realistic statistical power of studies, the low prior probability of tested hypotheses, and selective reporting of results, the majority of claims contained in published scientific papers in a given field may actually turn out to be false [1, p. 696].
What psychology, sociology, and medicine have in common in this context is the method of drawing conclusions—null hypothesis significance testing—which is why this crisis is not a series of local failures, but a systemic and, ultimately, epistemological problem regarding what exactly constitutes scientific evidence.
The first cause lies in the incentive system of academic institutions, aptly described by the phrase "publish or perish".
Since career advancement, funding, and prestige depend on the number of publications and journal rankings, and the publications themselves favor novel and statistically significant results rather than the absence of an effect or exact replications of previous studies, scientists have a rational incentive to obtain precisely those results that are least likely to be true [1, p. 697].
Consequently, the distortion that arises in the body of published work as a result is not only a methodological, but also an institutional and ethical problem.
The second cause is the misleading impression of objectivity provided by statistical calculations.
This is best seen in the example of fitting data to a desired result.
Statistical significance testing supposedly provides a clear numerical boundary showing whether an effect is real or not.
But in reality, researchers have great freedom: how to collect data, how to record it, how to analyze it.
Simmons, Nelson, and Simonsohn demonstrated through examples: even if each individual choice seems innocent, together they can raise the false-positive rate from the usual five percent to over sixty—and this does not require conscious deception [3, p. 1363].
The deeper problem here is philosophical: the measure of statistical significance only indicates how surprising the data would be if the effect did not exist at all.
It does not state whether the effect is real or whether it is robust.
Statistical significance has begun to replace professional judgment, rather than serving as its foundation.
The third, most distinctly philosophical cause requires revisiting Karl Popper's criterion of falsifiability.
Popper argued that the scientific nature of a theory is determined not by its verification, but by its openness to the logical risk of refutation [4, p. 41].
In the context of modern practice, this reveals a paradox: statistical significance testing looks outwardly like a search for refutations, but flexibility in choosing analytical methods and the reluctance to publish negative results mean that hypotheses rarely face a real risk of being refuted—instead, the search continues until a confirming result is obtained.
As Chalmers notes, Popper's criterion requires not only logical falsifiability but also a critical attitude, which implies active attempts to refute one's own hypotheses [6, p. 76].
Therefore, this crisis is not a crisis of Popper's idea itself, but of how it is applied in practice: the logic of hypothesis testing remains correct, but scientific institutions and statistical methods no longer support the spirit of seeking refutations that Popper considered the primary hallmark of science.
Given all this, the proposed measures represent a genuine paradigm shift in the sense of Thomas Kuhn: an accumulation of anomalies that can no longer be resolved through minor adjustments and which instead require a revision of the shared methodological foundations of the field [5, p. 92].
The "open science" movement embodies precisely such a shift: preregistration of hypotheses and analysis plans before data collection begins, "registered reports" evaluated on the quality of the study design rather than the obtained results, open access to data and software code, and a re-evaluation of the value of direct replication.
Nosek and colleagues explain: preregistration brings the idea of falsifiability back into practice, because it prevents retroactively adjusting the analysis to fit the result and thereby diminishing the true risk of refutation [7, p. 2601].
These reforms are not merely technical improvements; they are designed to restore at the level of everyday practice the critical spirit that Popper considered the defining feature of science.
In summary, it should be noted that the replication crisis is a well-documented practical problem in psychology, sociology, and medicine, yet its origins are epistemological in nature.
Its causes—the "publish or perish" incentive system, the misleading objectivity of easily manipulated statistics, and the decline of falsification practices—are distinct but mutually reinforcing factors; overcoming them requires not piecemeal fixes, but a shift in the shared methodological paradigm of empirical research towards transparency, openness to refutation, and genuine reproducibility of results.
